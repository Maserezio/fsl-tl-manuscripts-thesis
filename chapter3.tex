\chapter{Methodology}
\label{sec:meth}

Related work analysis identified three gaps. Few-shot \gls{tls} in historical manuscripts has been evaluated only on U-DIADS-TL, which provides three training pages per manuscript~\cite{Zottin_2025_FEST}, while for other collections the checked studies used their full training sets. Only one of the reviewed studies compares encoders, ResNet-50 with VGG-16 \cite{jindal2023fasterrcnn}, so modern encoders such as ConvNeXt or \glspl{vit} have not been compared. The initialization of the encoder has not been studied either, and distillation from vision foundation models and domain-specific pretraining have not been compared under the same setting, alongside with ways of turning features into line instances. \gls{rq}~1 therefore asks which encoder, initialization, and line reconstruction yield \gls{sota} few-shot \gls{tls}, and compares them in two pipeline types. With only a few training pages, it is unclear whether random selection of pages is sufficient or whether a dedicated selection strategy gives better results. \gls{rq}~2 therefore experiments with their selection to find how many annotated pages reach the \gls{sota}. Finally, transfer across collections has been studied only for models trained on full datasets, which leaves room for a few-shot model robustness study. \gls{rq}~3 therefore varies the number and and input of the source collections and evaluates the robustness on manuscript collections that were not seen during training.

\label{sec:meth-overview}
The study design follows the research questions. Therefore, the pipelines are built so that each factor can be varied independently, the encoder and its initialization on the input side and the line reconstruction on the output side. As described in Section~\ref{sec:related-supervised}, the reviewed methods follow two paradigms: semantic segmentation predicts which pixels belong to text lines and separates the lines afterwards, while instance segmentation detects every line as an object. It is unclear which of the two paradigms works better with a few annotated pages and which would deal better with overlapping lines of historical manuscripts. To compare both paradigms under the same conditions, this thesis builds two pipelines for each paradigm. For semantic segmentation, a U-Net with projection cuts and a center-line model adapted from ParseNet~\cite{kodym2021page} are described in Section~\ref{sec:meth-segmentation}. For instance segmentation, RT-DETR with a \gls{bbox} U-Net and Mask R-CNN are described in Section~\ref{sec:meth-detection}. All models are built on the same encoder families, so that the encoder architecture and its initialization can be varied independently of the pipeline. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline-overview} shows the structure shared by all pipelines. The manuscript collections differ in the format of their ground truth, hence the pipelines are built from interchangeable stages, so that a stage designed for one pipeline can complement another when a collection requires a different output.

\section{Encoders and Initializations}
\label{sec:backbones}

The encoders and their initializations define the design space of \gls{rq}~1. With only a few training pages, an encoder can learn little from the target collection, so the impact of initial weights needs to be assessed. Three architectures are compared, which process the page in different ways: a convolutional network, a hierarchical transformer, and a plain transformer. The two hierarchical encoders output feature maps at four resolutions, which the decoders use directly. The plain transformer outputs a single resolution, which is converted into a feature pyramid:

\label{sec:encoder-architectures}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ConvNeXt}~\cite{liu2022convnet2020s} is a convolutional network modernized with design elements of \glspl{vit}, such as large kernels and layer normalization. ConvNeXt-Tiny outputs feature maps with 96, 192, 384, and 768 channels at strides of 4, 8, 16, and 32 pixels.
    \item \textbf{PVTv2}~\cite{wang2022pvtv2}, the Pyramid Vision Transformer v2, computes attention over spatially reduced keys and values, so that it can process high-resolution feature maps. PVTv2-B2 outputs feature maps with 64, 128, 320, and 512 channels at the same strides.
    \item \textbf{\gls{vit}}~\cite{dosovitskiy2021imageworth16x16words} splits the page into patches of \(16\times16\) pixels and processes them with global self-attention at a single resolution. ViT-Small outputs one feature map with 384 channels at a stride of 16 pixels, from which transposed convolutions derive maps at strides of 4 and 8 pixels and max-pooling a map at a stride of 32 pixels.
\end{itemize}
Each encoder is fine-tuned from each of the initializations described below, so that the architecture and the initialization are varied as two separate factors. Each of the initializations is described below:

\label{sec:initializations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Random.} Weights drawn with the default initialization of the respective framework, so that the encoder learns its features only from the few training pages.
    \item \textbf{ImageNet.} Weights from supervised image classification on ImageNet~\cite{deng2009imagenet}.
    \item \textbf{DINOv3 (LVD-1689M).} Weights released by Meta, distilled from the frozen DINOv3 ViT-7B on 1.7 billion web images (LVD-1689M)~\cite{dinov3}, where the student learns with the self-supervised DINO objective to match the outputs of the teacher on different views of the same image~\cite{caron2021dino}. They exist for ConvNeXt-Tiny and ViT-Small, but not for PVTv2, hence PVTv2-B2 has fewer encoder and initialization combinations than the other encoders.
    \item \textbf{DINOv3 (cBAD).} Weights obtained by knowledge distillation on the unlabeled page images of cBAD 2019~\cite{cbad-2019}. A frozen DINOv3 ViT-B/16 serves as the teacher, and the encoder of the pipeline is the student. The student starts from random weights and is trained to reproduce the feature map of the teacher on random crops of the pages~\cite{beyer2022knowledgedistillationgoodteacher}. In this way, it learns features of document images without any annotations.
    \item \textbf{CATMuS.} Weights pretrained with supervision on the line polygons of pages of CATMuS Medieval~\cite{catmus-medieval}, which is supervised task-specific transfer learning~\cite{tian2020rethinking}. 
\end{itemize}

\label{sec:encoder-size}

The encoders are grouped by parameter count into three brackets: XS (fewer than 8M parameters), S (8M to 20M parameters), and M (more than 20M parameters), and are listed in Table~\ref{tab:backbones}. The main comparison uses the M bracket, namely ConvNeXt-Tiny, PVTv2-B2, and ViT-Small. The XS and S brackets are evaluated only in an encoder-size study on U-DIADS-TL, which is reported in Appendix~\ref{app:rq1-size}.

\section{Line Prediction and Reconstruction}
\label{sec:pipelines}\label{sec:reconstruction}

As described in Section~\ref{sec:related-supervised}, the reviewed \gls{tls} methods follow two main paradigms: semantic segmentation classifies every pixel of the page and forms the individual lines only in the line reconstruction, and instance segmentation detects every line as a separate object and therefore forms the lines in the prediction, as one box per line. This thesis evaluates both paradigms in the few-shot setting to determine which of them works better with a low amount of annotated training pages. This section describes what the networks of both paradigms predict and how the individual lines are reconstructed from these predictions.

\subsection{Instance Segmentation}
\label{sec:meth-detection}

In this approach, every text line is treated as an object. A detector first predicts one \gls{bbox} per text line, and a second stage then decides which pixels inside each box belong to the line. Since neighboring lines are separated by their boxes,
the second stage only determines the extent of each line. RT-DETR and BBox U-Net make this split explicit, so that the detection and the mask can be evaluated and exchanged separately. This split led to \gls{sota} results on U-DIADS-TL. Figure~\ref{fig:two-stage-method} shows how both detectors and segmentors are combined with the second stage.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \input{graphics/detection_gt_workflow}
    \caption{Instance segmentation pipelines. The \glsentryshort{dp} seam (dashed) exports the mask of each box.}
    \label{fig:two-stage-method}\label{fig:mask-rcnn-architecture}
\end{figure}

RT-DETR with BBox Segmentation \label{sec:two-stage-method}\label{sec:crop-roi}\label{sec:crop-unet}\label{sec:integration}\label{sec:boundary-heads} uses RT-DETR~\cite{Zhao_2024_CVPR} to predict one \gls{bbox} per text line, and the BBox U-Net predicts the mask of the line inside each box. The confidence threshold is selected on the validation split, and duplicate detections are removed. A separate segmentation model in each box can adapt better to the \gls{gt} of a collection, which motivates the combination of separate detector and segmenter.

The BBox U-Net is a U-Net~\cite{DBLP:journals/corr/RonnebergerFB15} that segments each line crop. The training targets of it are derived from the \gls{gt}: each PAGE XML polygon, or each component of a binary mask, defines a \gls{bbox}, and the line mask inside this box forms the target. For binary masks, only the selected component is retained. The box is padded to keep context, and both crop and target are resized to a common input size.

At inference, each retained detection is padded in the same way and segmented independently. The probability map is resized to the native crop size before thresholding, and only its main connected component is kept. For binary-mask datasets, a horizontal morphological closing can additionally join fragmented parts of a line. The binarization threshold and the closing width are selected per detector on the validation split by the mean line-level \gls{fm}, in the same way as the detection threshold.

Mask R-CNN\label{sec:mask-rcnn-method}~\cite{he2017maskrcnn} predicts one box and a coarse \(28\times28\) mask per line. The confidence threshold is selected on the validation split by minimizing the absolute error of the predicted line count, and the mask threshold is fixed at 0.5 by default. The mask is predicted by the \gls{roi} head, the mask branch of Mask R-CNN.

For datasets with binary mask ground truth format the coarse \gls{roi} masks do not match, since a \(28\times28\) mask predicts a polygon. For this reason, the \gls{roi} masks are replaced by the BBox U-Net, while Mask R-CNN still supplies the proposals, their scores, and the instance identities. As shown in Appendix~\ref{app:training-settings}, its operating points are selected on the validation split. In this configuration, only the box branch of Mask R-CNN is used at inference, which corresponds to Faster R-CNN~\cite{ren2017fasterrcnn} with a feature pyramid network and RoIAlign~\cite{he2017maskrcnn}. Its weights, however, result from joint training with the mask branch.

Figure~\ref{fig:two-stage-example} illustrates both detectors on one page. For RT-DETR, the region and width filters reduce 289 raw detections to 33.

\label{sec:export}In the instance segmentation pipelines, every accepted box yields one candidate line, and the page-level output is assembled in descending detection confidence by rules that depend on the \gls{gt} format. For binary-mask \gls{gt}, the masks are pasted into an empty page-sized instance map, and pixels that are already assigned are not overwritten. For PAGE XML format, the largest contour of each mask becomes the line polygon, and the baseline is derived from its lower boundary. As a consequence, parts of a line that are disconnected in the mask might be discarded, for example at a wide word gap, as shown by the red parts in Figure~\ref{fig:dp-seam-example-a}.

Dynamic-Programming Seam \label{sec:dp-seam} is a step that replaces this largest-contour export for the mask of each box, from the BBox U-Net or the \gls{roi} head. Instead of the largest contour, it derives a line region that is connected by construction, as shown in Algorithm~\ref{alg:dp-seam}. The method adapts seam carving~\cite{avidan2007seamcarving}, which finds minimum-energy paths through an image by \gls{dp} and has been used to separate text lines on full pages~\cite{saabni2014textline, arvanitopoulos2014seamcarving, nguyen2022effective}. It follows the medial and separating seams of Saabni et al.~\cite{saabni2014textline} and Arvanitopoulos and S\"usstrunk~\cite{arvanitopoulos2014seamcarving}, but computes them on the mask probability map \(P\) of one box instead of the page image. In every column, a center row takes the role of the medial seam, and an upper and a lower path bound the line. Together, the two paths maximize the sum of \(P-t\) over the pixels between them, where \(t\) is the mask threshold, and each path may move by at most \(K\) rows between neighboring columns. For PAGE XML, the region between the paths is exported as the line polygon, while for binary masks it is intersected with the thresholded crop mask. Figure~\ref{fig:dp-seam-example} illustrates the steps on a line where the largest contour discards 69\% of the thresholded mask.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
    \small
    \caption{\Gls{dp} seam for one detected line, adapted from the medial and separating seams of~\cite{saabni2014textline, arvanitopoulos2014seamcarving}}
    \label{alg:dp-seam}
    \KwIn{Crop probability map \(P\), mask threshold \(t\), maximum step \(K\)}
    \KwOut{Line region between an upper and a lower path}
    Average \(P\) over columns of four pixels and keep the columns between the first and the last column whose maximum is at least \(t\)\;
    \(c_x \leftarrow\) row of the maximum of the vertically smoothed \(P\) in column \(x\)\;
    Interpolate \(c\) in columns whose maximum is below \(t\)\;
    \(u \leftarrow \arg\max_{u} \sum_{x}\sum_{y=u_x}^{c_x} (P_{y,x}-t)\) subject to \(u_x \le c_x\) and \(|u_x-u_{x-1}| \le K\)\;
    \(l \leftarrow \arg\max_{l} \sum_{x}\sum_{y=c_x}^{l_x} (P_{y,x}-t)\) subject to \(l_x \ge c_x\) and \(|l_x-l_{x-1}| \le K\)\;
    \Return{the region between \(u\) and \(l\)}\;
\end{algorithm}


\subsection{Semantic Segmentation}
\label{sec:meth-segmentation}\label{sec:tls-methods}\label{sec:rq3-center-line}

Both semantic segmentation pipelines use a U-Net~\cite{DBLP:journals/corr/RonnebergerFB15} model. The designed pipelines differ only in the outputs that the U-Net learns to predict. Figure~\ref{fig:semantic-method} shows an overview of both pipelines.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{\input{graphics/semantic_workflow}}
    \caption{Semantic segmentation pipelines on U-DIADS-TL Latin14396 test page 032. Center line (red), line ends (blue), distances to the line boundaries (green), and ARU-Net baselines~\cite{gruning2019arunet} (red, top).}
    \label{fig:semantic-method}
\end{figure}

\label{sec:semantic-postprocessing}The first pipeline, a U-Net with a text line mask, predicts the probability of pixel belonging to a text line. It is trained on a binary mask of the annotated text lines with a sum of binary cross-entropy and Dice loss. As shown in Algorithm~\ref{alg:semantic-postprocessing}, projection cuts then split the probability map into line instances. First, the map is binarized, and small components are removed. For each remaining component, the row-wise pixel sum forms a horizontal projection, in which peaks mark candidate line centers and a sufficiently deep valley between two peaks marks a cut position. The prediction and these steps are the same for all datasets. Only the cuts and the export are adapted to the format of the \gls{gt}, which differs between the benchmarks, while the \gls{gt} itself is not used at inference. If the ground truth is a binary line mask, the baseline prediction of ARU-Net~\cite{gruning2019arunet} is binarized with Otsu's method~\cite{otsu1979threshold}, thinned, and combined with the mask. The cuts then follow minimum-energy seams~\cite{avidan2007seamcarving, saabni2014textline}, which pass through the background between two lines. If the ground truth consists of PAGE XML polygons, morphological closing first joins fragmented characters and words. The lines are then separated by straight horizontal cuts and exported. Figure~\ref{fig:semantic-method} shows the mask and the resulting lines.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
    \small
    \caption{Postprocessing of a semantic text line probability map}
    \label{alg:semantic-postprocessing}
    \KwIn{Probability map \(P\), page image \(I\), output format \(F\) of the benchmark (only the format, no ground-truth content), and main text-region mask \(R\), when available}
    \KwOut{Individual text line components}

    \(M \leftarrow\) binarize \(P\) and remove small foreground components\;

    \eIf{\(F=\text{binary mask}\)}{
        \(B \leftarrow\) apply Otsu thresholding to the ARU-Net baseline prediction for \(I\)\;
        Thin \(B\), combine it with \(M\), and remove small components\;
        \(C \leftarrow\) minimum-energy seam\;
    }{
        Join character and word fragments using morphological closing\;
        \(C \leftarrow\) straight horizontal cut\;
    }

    Find projection peaks and valleys in each connected component of \(M\)\;
    Separate merged lines through accepted valleys using \(C\)\;
    Remove small fragments created by the cuts\;

    \eIf{\(F=\text{binary mask}\)}{
        \Return{the connected components of \(M\) as text line instances}\;
    }{
        Clip \(M\) to \(R\)\;
        Discard components that are too small, too short, or insufficiently wide\;
        Convert the remaining components to polygons and baselines in PAGE XML\;
        \Return{the PAGE XML text line polygons}\;
    }
\end{algorithm}


The second pipeline, the center-line model, adapts ParseNet~\cite{kodym2021page} and differs from it in two points. First, it predicts the center line instead of the baseline, which was motivated by the \gls{gt} of U-DIADS-TL where the target lines are struck through. The center line lies between the upper and lower boundary of the line region and can therefore be derived from any line region without a baseline annotation. Second, it does not construct a polygon of constant height around each line. Instead, its line reconstruction depends on the \gls{gt} format of the collection. \label{sec:center-line-decoding}In both cases, the thresholded center map, reduced by the end map as in ParseNet, is first split into fragments. The fragments are joined into lines when their vertical offset is small relative to the line pitch \(p\), the median vertical distance between neighboring fragments. Appendix~\ref{app:training-settings} lists all settings and thresholds of the center-line model.

For \gls{gt} with line polygons, the pixels of each line are determined by ink ownership. The ink is obtained by Otsu~\cite{otsu1979threshold} thresholding of the background-normalized page, so that no \gls{gt} is used. An ink component that touches exactly one center line belongs to this line, and a component that touches several center lines is split between them along the minimum-ink seam, which is computed by \gls{dp} as in Section~\ref{sec:dp-seam}.

Binary-mask \gls{gt} marks every line as one connected region that encloses its ink strokes, which cannot be obtained from a binarization of the page. Therefore, the band between the predicted upper and lower distances locates each line, as in ParseNet, and the box around it is passed to the BBox U-Net, which predicts the line mask.

\section{Shot Selection Methods}
\label{sec:shot-sampling}

The second part of \gls{rq}~2 asks whether the choice of the few training pages matters. With a small, fixed training budget, the choice of pages determines which layouts and visual conditions the model sees during training. Therefore, the page-count study compares page selection strategies while holding the number of annotated training pages fixed. Unless stated otherwise, the experiments use random selection.

Each strategy chooses \(k\) pages from a pool of \(N\) training candidates. Four of them work on a two-dimensional embedding of the pages. The 512-dimensional full-page features \(\mathbf{z}_i\) of an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18 without its classification layer~\cite{He_2016_CVPR} are reduced to two dimensions, representing each page as a point \(\mathbf{y}_i \in \mathbb{R}^2\), by one of two methods:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \Gls{pca} projects the features onto the two eigenvectors of the sample covariance matrix with the largest eigenvalues. These vectors form an orthonormal basis for the selected two-dimensional subspace, which retains the maximum possible variance. The resulting components are uncorrelated, but not necessarily statistically independent~\cite{Jolliffe2016PCA}.
    \item \Gls{ica}, computed with FastICA, seeks two statistically independent components by maximizing non-Gaussianity after whitening, which removes correlations and scales the retained components to unit variance. Its objective is independence rather than maximum retained variance, so the resulting directions need not be orthogonal in the original feature space, and the components are not ordered by explained variance~\cite{Hyvarinen1999FastICA}.
\end{itemize}
The six strategies are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Random sampling} selects \(k\) pages without replacement from the lexicographically sorted candidates.
    \item \emph{Grayscale variance} selects the \(k\) pages with the largest score
    \[
        v_i = \frac{1}{H_i W_i}
        \sum_{x=1}^{W_i}\sum_{y=1}^{H_i}
        \left(I_i(x,y)-\bar{I}_i\right)^2,
    \]
    where \(H_i\), \(W_i\), and \(\bar{I}_i\) denote the height, width, and mean intensity of page \(I_i\).
    \item \emph{\gls{pca} max--min} and \emph{\gls{ica} max--min} select, in the respective embedding, the size-\(k\) subset with the largest minimum pairwise distance,
    \[
        S_{\mathrm{max\text{-}min}}^{*}
        = \underset{S \subseteq \{1,\ldots,N\},\,|S|=k}{\operatorname{arg\,max}}
          \;\underset{\substack{i,j\in S\\i\neq j}}{\operatorname{min}}
          \lVert \mathbf{y}_i-\mathbf{y}_j \rVert_2.
    \]
    For \(k=1\), all singleton scores are zero, so the first sorted page is returned.
    \item \emph{\gls{pca} centroid} and \emph{\gls{ica} centroid} select the \(k\) pages farthest from the embedding centroid,
    \[
        \bar{\mathbf{y}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{y}_i,
        \qquad
        S_{\mathrm{centroid}}^{*}
        = \underset{S \subseteq \{1,\ldots,N\},\,|S|=k}{\operatorname{arg\,max}}
          \sum_{i\in S}\lVert\mathbf{y}_i-\bar{\mathbf{y}}\rVert_2.
    \]
\end{itemize}
The max--min rule maximizes the mutual separation of the selected pages, while the centroid rule selects peripheral pages independently of each other.

\section{Cross-Collection Transfer Settings}
\label{sec:meth-transfer}

\Gls{rq}~3 examines whether a model trained on a few pages of one or more collections is robust enough to transfer to a collection that it has never seen during training. Fine-tuning on a few annotated pages adapts a pretrained model to these pages but can reduce the robustness that it gained during pretraining. Therefore, it is checked whether \gls{wiseft}~\cite{wortsman2022wiseft} improves the transfer. The weights of the pretrained and the fine-tuned model are linearly interpolated,
\begin{equation}
    \theta = (1-\alpha)\,\theta_{\mathrm{pre}} + \alpha\,\theta_{\mathrm{ft}},
    \label{eq:wiseft}
\end{equation}
where \(\theta_{\mathrm{pre}}\) and \(\theta_{\mathrm{ft}}\) denote the pretrained and fine-tuned weights, and \(\alpha\) is the interpolation weight. A weight of \(\alpha=0\) gives the pretrained model and \(\alpha=1\) the fine-tuned model. T
Transfer is measured in five settings:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Without fine-tuning.} The model is applied without fine-tuning, using the fixed thresholds.
    \item \textbf{Single-collection transfer.} One model is trained on the three training pages of each collection, its thresholds are selected on the validation pages of that collection, and it is applied unchanged to the test sets of all collections. The diagonal of the resulting matrix is the in-domain result, and the off-diagonal cells measure the transfer to collections that were not seen during training.
    \item \textbf{\Gls{loco} transfer.} For each held-out collection, a \gls{loco} model is trained on \(k\in\{1,2,3\}\) pages from each of the other collections (\gls{loco}-1, \gls{loco}-2, and \gls{loco}-3). The page sets are nested and taken from the single-collection training pages. The thresholds are selected on up to three validation pages per source collection, so the held-out collection takes no part in training or selection. The result is compared with the in-domain result and with the best off-diagonal single-collection entry of the same column, which overestimates a source chosen in advance because it is identified on test results.
    \item \textbf{Continuous hold-out transfer.} Between the \gls{loco} and the single-collection setting, the collections are held out one after another in order to avoid conclusions made in a discrete setup. The first held-out collection is the one whose pages differ most from the other collections. In this case the ImageNet ResNet-18 page features of Section~\ref{sec:shot-sampling} are computed for the training and validation pages of all collections, standardized, and averaged per collection, and the collection means are projected on their first principal component. The furthest collection from the mean of all collections on this component is held out first. Starting from \gls{loco}-3 with all other collections as sources, one more source collection is held out at each step, ordered by its distance to the first held-out collection on this component, until a single source collection remains. Two orders are compared, holding out either the most distant or the nearest source collection first. Every step uses the \gls{loco}-3 training and validation pages of the remaining source collections, and every model is evaluated on the test sets of all collections.
    \item \textbf{Fixed page budget.} The \gls{loco} setting varies the number of source pages but not the number of source collections. Therefore, the number of training pages is fixed to six, and these pages are split over six, three, or two source collections with one, two, or three pages each. The source collections and their pages are drawn with the seed from the other collections and their single-collection training pages. As for \gls{loco}, up to three validation pages per source collection are used for threshold selection, and the held-out collection takes no part in training or selection. Every split is repeated with three seeds.
\end{itemize}
