\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:intro}

\Gls{tls} is an important step in document analysis, since its quality affects downstream \gls{ocr} and \gls{htr} results \cite{fizaine}. For historical manuscripts, this task is more challenging than for printed documents for two reasons: physical degradation, such as ink bleeding and faded sections \cite{Rabaev_Litvak_2025}, and varying writing styles within and between manuscript collections, text orientation, and overlapping lines and characters \cite{diva_hisdb}.

Labeled data for historical manuscripts are scarce, since labeling is a costly and time-consuming process that requires expert knowledge \cite{sterzinger2025fewshot}. For printed historical newspapers, Liebl and Burghardt \cite{liebl2021pagesegmentation} report that the pixel-level layout annotation took one to four hours per page. The public \gls{tls} benchmarks of manuscripts are correspondingly small, with 20 training pages per manuscript in DIVA-HisDB \cite{diva_hisdb} and three in U-DIADS-TL \cite{Zottin_2025_FEST}. Given this lack of labeled data, the \gls{fsl} paradigm can be employed.

\Gls{fsl} is a machine learning paradigm that aims to train models using a small number of labeled examples (shots) per class \cite{fsl}, where the number of shots usually ranges from 1 to 10. \Gls{fsl} typically relies on two main strategies: meta-learning, which teaches a model how to learn and quickly adapt to new tasks \cite{finn2017modelagnosticmetalearningfastadaptation}, and transfer learning, where a model pretrained on a large source dataset is fine-tuned for a specific task using limited labeled data \cite{trans_learning}. In \gls{hda}, large source datasets typically contain about 1,500--3,000 annotated document images, such as \gls{cbad} with 3,021 annotated pages \cite{DiemKleberGatos2019} and CATMuS Medieval with 1,705 annotated images \cite{catmus-medieval}. In comparison, ImageNet contains more than 14 million annotated images across over 20,000 object categories \cite{deng2009imagenet}.

For text line segmentation, \gls{fsl} models with transfer learning can rely on pretrained encoders, such as ResNet, which have been shown to outperform older backbones like VGG-16 in text line segmentation, as shown by the ResNet-50-based Faster R-CNN \cite{jindal2023fasterrcnn}.
\Glspl{vit}, as a next step in computer vision, have been shown to outperform them on large-scale recognition benchmarks \cite{dosovitskiy2021imageworth16x16words}, and can be trained on ImageNet without additional training data \cite{touvron2021trainingdataefficientimagetransformers}. Later, convolutional networks such as ConvNeXt, which adopt the training procedure of \glspl{vit} (longer training, the AdamW optimizer, and stronger data augmentation) and some of their design elements, such as larger kernels and layer normalization, have been reported to achieve comparable performance \cite{liu2022convnet2020s}. More recently, vision foundation models pretrained with \gls{ssl} on large unlabeled image collections have become an alternative to supervised pretraining. For instance, distillation from self-supervised foundation models such as DINOv3 has outperformed standard supervised pretraining on dense tasks such as semantic segmentation \cite{dinov3}. These developments and the constraints of \gls{tls} raise the question of which backbone and which training paradigm, such as pretraining or knowledge distillation followed by fine-tuning on the target collection, are most effective for few-shot \gls{tls} in historical manuscripts, and how much the performance depends on the initialization of the backbone. This is particularly relevant given that domain-specific pretraining has been shown to improve performance in the related task of document layout analysis \cite{denardin2025indomain}.

Apart from a powerful feature extractor, a \gls{tls} model must assign text pixels to individual lines, for example by extracting lines from a pixel-level prediction in a postprocessing step, reconstructing them from predicted baselines, or treating each line as an object~\cite{Rabaev_Litvak_2025}. This step, referred to as line reconstruction in this thesis, depends on the ground-truth format, such as line polygons or baselines, and may depend on the line structure of the manuscript, for example when neighboring lines approach or overlap. It is therefore unclear whether few-shot \gls{tls} is limited by the feature extractor or by the line reconstruction step. This thesis presents a systematic study of few-shot \gls{tls} in historical manuscripts across different encoders, initializations, and approaches for line localization and reconstruction, with different numbers of training pages, and evaluates the robustness of the models on diverse unseen manuscript collections under different setups.


\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem-statement}

Few-shot \gls{tls} of historical manuscripts faces the following challenges:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Encoder, Initialization, and Line Reconstruction:} Encoder architectures are usually compared on large natural-image benchmarks. It remains unclear, in terms of \gls{tls} metrics, which architecture and which priors---generic or domain-specific pretraining, \gls{ssl} distillation on manuscript data---provide the most useful features when only a few pages are annotated. Additionally, a powerful feature extractor may not produce better lines, since the limitation may be in the line reconstruction step that turns predictions into separate lines.
	\item \textbf{Data Amount Sensitivity:} Existing \gls{tls} models for historical manuscripts are trained on complete datasets. Few-shot methods have so far been evaluated on U-DIADS-TL with three training pages \cite{zottin2025exploring, Zottin_2025_FEST}. The minimum number of pages needed for \gls{sota} performance has not been evaluated.
	\item \textbf{Cross-Collection Generalization:} A model trained on one collection may fail on collections with other scripts, layouts, and writing styles. It remains unclear whether a few annotated pages from several collections or pretraining on related data are sufficient for transfer to a new collection.
\end{itemize}



\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:rqs}\label{ch:rqs}

Based on a gap in \gls{tls} for historical manuscripts under limited data constraints, the following \glspl{rq} were formulated:

\paragraph{RQ1:} How do the encoder architecture, its initialization, and the line reconstruction affect few-shot text line segmentation in historical manuscripts and yield \gls{sota} results in terms of line detection metrics?

\paragraph{RQ2:} How many labeled pages are needed to achieve performance comparable to \gls{sota} methods in terms of line detection metrics?

\paragraph{RQ3:} How does the diversity of the training collections in script and layout affect the robustness of a few-shot text line segmentation model on unseen manuscript collections?

Compared with the thesis proposal, \gls{rq}~1 additionally covers the initialization and the line reconstruction. \Gls{rq}~3 examines the diversity of scripts and layouts instead of the diversity of scripts and the degree of degradation of the training documents.


\section{Contributions}

The main contributions of this thesis are:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Systematic Few-Shot Factor Analysis with \gls{sota} Results:} A comparison of few-shot \gls{tls} with two types of pipelines, semantic segmentation followed by line reconstruction and instance segmentation. Within these pipelines, convolutional and transformer-based encoders of different sizes are evaluated with up to five initializations on U-DIADS-TL and DIVA-HisDB: random weights, supervised pretraining on ImageNet, self-supervised DINOv3 pretraining on natural images, knowledge distillation from DINOv3 on \gls{cbad} document images, and supervised pretraining on the domain-specific dataset with line reconstruction step analyzed separately from the encoder. For the same Mask R-CNN detections on U-DIADS-TL, the bounding box U-Net raises the line \gls{fm} from 16.6\% to 90.8\%. The best configurations reach 92.2\% on U-DIADS-TL and 99.2\% on DIVA-HisDB compared with the published 83.0\%~\cite{Zottin_2025_FEST} and 97.4\%~\cite{signals3030032}.
	
	\item \textbf{Data Amount Sensitivity Study with \gls{sota} Results with One Page:} A study of the number of training pages, from one to twenty pages per manuscript. With pretraining on annotated manuscript lines, a single training page per manuscript reaches a line \gls{fm} of up to 97.7\% on DIVA-HisDB, compared with the published 97.4\% with all 20 training pages~\cite{signals3030032}. The single-page result depends on the selected page and is 95.4\(\pm\)3.6\% over three seeds, and three pages yield 98.7\(\pm\)0.3\%.
	
	\item \textbf{Cross-Collection Transfer Study:} An evaluation of transfer across seven manuscript collections with different scripts and layouts, without fine-tuning, from a single source collection, and from the six other collections. A continuous hold-out experiment removes the source collections one after another, and a fixed budget of six training pages distributed over two, three, or six source collections varies the diversity of the sources without changing the number of training pages. Both models transfer to unseen collections when they are trained on the six other collections, with a mean line \gls{fm} of 93.4\% for the center-line model and 85.4\% for Mask R-CNN. Without any pages from the target collection, the center-line model reaches 93.1\%. With the same six training pages, the number of source collections changes the result of the center-line model by less than one percentage point, while Mask R-CNN drops from 83.6\% with six source collections to 77.6\% with three on average over three seeds.
\end{itemize}



\section{Thesis Structure}

Chapter~\ref{lit_rec} reviews text line segmentation methods, few-shot and transfer learning, and the datasets used in this thesis. Chapter~\ref{sec:meth} identifies the research gap and describes the evaluated line segmentation pipelines, the encoders and their initializations, the page selection methods, and the evaluation metrics. Chapter~\ref{ch:results} describes the datasets, their annotation formats and partitions, the ground-truth conversion, and the training and transfer settings. It then presents the encoder and initialization comparison, the line reconstruction analysis, the experiments with different numbers of training pages, and the cross-collection evaluation, each with an analysis of the errors. Section~\ref{metrics} describes the line-detection metrics mentioned in \gls{rq}s and used for evaluation. \Gls{rq}~1 is answered in Sections~\ref{sec:results-models} and~\ref{sec:results-reconstruction}, \gls{rq}~2 in Section~\ref{sec:results-rq2}, and \gls{rq}~3 in Section~\ref{sec:results-rq3}. Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} relates the results across the research questions and states the limitations of the study. Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions} answers the research questions, summarizes the findings, and outlines directions for future work.
