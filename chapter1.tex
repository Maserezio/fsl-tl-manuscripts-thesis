\chapter{Introduction}

This chapter motivates few-shot text line segmentation of historical manuscripts, describes the problems that it raises, and formulates the research questions. It then summarizes the contributions of this thesis and outlines its structure.

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

\Gls{tls} is an important step in document analysis, since its quality affects downstream \gls{ocr} and \gls{htr} results \cite{fizaine}. For historical manuscripts, this task is more challenging than for printed documents for two reasons: physical degradation, such as ink bleeding and faded sections \cite{Rabaev_Litvak_2025}, and varying writing styles within and between manuscript collections, text orientation, and overlapping lines and characters \cite{diva_hisdb}.

Many manuscripts are digitized as images and available online. However, labeling is a costly and time-consuming process. For example, Tarride et al. \cite{tarride2023handwrittentextrecognitioncrowdsourced} report that, on average, a crowdsourced annotator took 21 minutes to annotate a full page. Given the cost of obtaining labeled data for large manuscript collections, the \gls{fsl} paradigm can be employed.

\Gls{fsl} is a machine learning paradigm that aims to train models using a small number of labeled examples (shots) per class \cite{fsl}, where the number of shots usually ranges from 1 to 10. \gls{fsl} typically relies on two main strategies: meta-learning, which teaches a model how to learn and quickly adapt to new tasks \cite{finn2017modelagnosticmetalearningfastadaptation}, and transfer learning, where a model pretrained on a large source dataset is fine-tuned for a specific task using limited labeled data \cite{trans_learning}. In \gls{hda}, large source datasets typically contain about 1,500--3,000 annotated document images, such as \gls{cbad} with 3,021 annotated pages \cite{DiemKleberGatos2019} and CATMuS Medieval with 1,705 annotated images \cite{catmus-medieval}. In comparison, ImageNet contains more than 14 million annotated images across over 20,000 object categories \cite{deng2009imagenet}.

For text line segmentation, \gls{fsl} models with transfer learning can rely on pretrained encoders, such as ResNet, which have been shown to outperform older backbones like VGG-16 in text line segmentation, as shown by the ResNet-50-based Faster R-CNN \cite{jindal2023fasterrcnn}.
\Glspl{vit}, as a next step in computer vision, have been shown to outperform them on large-scale recognition benchmarks \cite{dosovitskiy2021imageworth16x16words}, even without large amounts of training data \cite{touvron2021trainingdataefficientimagetransformers}. Later, convolutional networks trained with modern methods have been reported to achieve comparable performance \cite{liu2022convnet2020s}. More recently, self-supervised vision foundation models have risen as a worthy alternative. For instance, distillation from self-supervised foundation models such as DINOv3 has outperformed standard supervised pretraining on dense tasks such as semantic segmentation \cite{dinov3}. These developments and the constraints of \gls{tls} raise the question of which backbone is most effective for few-shot \gls{tls} in historical manuscripts, and how much performance depends on the backbone's prior knowledge. This is particularly relevant given that domain-specific pretraining has been shown to improve performance in the related task of document layout analysis \cite{denardin2025indomain}.

Apart from a powerful feature extractor, a \gls{tls} model must assign text pixels to individual lines, for example by extracting lines from a pixel-level prediction in a postprocessing step, reconstructing them from predicted baselines, or treating each line as an object~\cite{Rabaev_Litvak_2025}. This step depends on the ground-truth format, and is most difficult when neighboring lines approach or overlap. It is therefore unclear whether few-shot \gls{tls} is limited by the encoder's features or by the step that turns its predictions into lines.


\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem-statement}

Few-shot \gls{tls} of historical manuscripts faces the following challenges:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Backbone and Initialization Choice:} Encoder architectures are usually compared on large natural-image benchmarks. It remains unclear, in terms of \gls{tls} metrics, which architecture and which priors---generic or domain-specific pretraining, \gls{ssl} distillation on manuscript data---provide the most useful features when only a few pages are annotated.
	\item \textbf{Line Reconstruction:} A powerful feature extractor may not produce better lines, since the limitation may be in the postprocessing/reconstruction step that turns predictions into separate lines, which also depends on the ground-truth format.
	\item \textbf{Data Amount Sensitivity:} Existing \gls{tls} models for historical manuscripts are trained on complete datasets. Few-shot \gls{tls} has so far been evaluated only on U-DIADS-TL with three training pages \cite{zottin2025exploring, Zottin_2025_FEST}, so the number of pages needed on other benchmarks is unknown.
	\item \textbf{Cross-Collection Generalization:} A model trained on one collection may fail on collections with other scripts, layouts, and writing styles. It remains unclear whether a few annotated pages from several collections or pretraining on related data are sufficient for transfer to a new collection, or which collection properties make transfer difficult.
\end{itemize}



\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:rqs}\label{ch:rqs}

Based on a gap in \gls{tls} for historical manuscripts under low data constraints, the following \glspl{rq} were formulated:

\paragraph{RQ1:} How well, in terms of the line detection metrics, does an \gls{fsl} model using a transformer-based backbone perform compared to one using a \gls{cnn} backbone for text line segmentation in historical manuscripts?

\paragraph{RQ2:} How many labeled images are needed to achieve performance comparable to \gls{sota} methods, as measured by the line detection metrics?

\paragraph{RQ3:} How do the heterogeneity and quality (e.g., Latin-only vs. mixed scripts; clean vs. mixed clean and degraded vs. only degraded) of training documents affect the performance of a text line segmentation model, i.e., does manuscript diversity impact \gls{fsl} model robustness?


Section~\ref {metrics} describes the line-detection metrics mentioned in \gls{rq}~1 and used for evaluation.
\Gls{rq}~1 is answered in Sections~\ref{sec:results-models} and~\ref{sec:results-reconstruction}, \gls{rq}~2 in Section~\ref{sec:results-rq2}, and \gls{rq}~3 in Section~\ref{sec:results-rq3}; Section~\ref{sec:disc-answers} discusses the answers.

\section{Contributions}

The main contributions of this thesis are:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Systematic Few-Shot Study:} A systematic comparison of few-shot \gls{tls} across two types of pipelines, semantic segmentation followed by postprocessing and instance segmentation, including different postprocessing methods. Within these pipelines, convolutional and transformer-based feature extractors of different sizes are evaluated with up to five initializations, from one to twenty annotated pages per manuscript, on U-DIADS-TL and DIVA-HisDB.
	\item \textbf{Postprocessing Analysis:} An analysis of the step that turns predictions into individual lines, separately from the features. For the same detections on U-DIADS-TL, the reconstruction stage changes the line \gls{fm} from 16.6\% with the native Mask R-CNN masks to 90.8\% with a separate crop segmentation network.
	\item \textbf{State-of-the-Art Results with Few Annotated Pages:} On U-DIADS-TL, the best configuration reaches a line \gls{fm} of 92.0\% with three training pages, compared with 83.0\% for the best published result~\cite{Zottin_2025_FEST}. On DIVA-HisDB, the best configuration reaches 99.2\%, above the published 97.4\%~\cite{signals3030032}. With pretraining on annotated manuscript lines, a single training page per manuscript yields 96.5\%. A \gls{dp} seam that keeps every line connected raises the single-page result to 98.9\%.
	\item \textbf{Robust Cross-Collection Transfer:} An evaluation of transfer across seven manuscript collections with different scripts and layouts. Without any pages from the target collection, the center-line model reaches 91.7\% mean line \gls{fm}, compared with 44.3\% for Mask R-CNN, and maintains this level when trained on other collections.
\end{itemize}



\section{Thesis Structure}

The remainder of the thesis is organized as follows:

\paragraph{Chapter~\ref{lit_rec}: Background and Related Work} reviews text line segmentation methods, few-shot and transfer learning, and the datasets used in this thesis, and identifies the research gap.

\paragraph{Chapter~\ref{sec:meth}: Methodology} describes the evaluated line segmentation pipelines, the feature extractors and their initializations, the page selection methods, and the evaluation metrics.

\paragraph{Chapter~\ref{ch:data}: Experimental Setup} describes the datasets, their annotation formats and partitions, the ground-truth conversion, and the training and transfer settings.

\paragraph{Chapter~\ref{ch:results}: Experiments and Results} presents the encoder and initialization comparison, the line reconstruction analysis, experiments with different numbers of training pages, and the cross-collection evaluation.

\paragraph{Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}: Discussion} interprets the results and answers the research questions.

\paragraph{Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions}: Conclusion and Future Work} summarizes the findings and outlines directions for future work.
